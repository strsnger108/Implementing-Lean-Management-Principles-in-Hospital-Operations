## ABSTRACT

**Background:** Healthcare systems across the globe face mounting pressure to deliver high-quality care while simultaneously controlling operational costs and reducing inefficiencies. Lean management, originally conceived within the Toyota Production System, has emerged as a transformative methodology for eliminating waste, streamlining processes, and enhancing value from the patient's perspective. Despite substantial evidence of its effectiveness in Western healthcare contexts, the application of Lean principles in Indian hospital settings remains underexplored, particularly in tier-two cities.

**Objective:** This study investigates the implementation of Lean management principles in the operations of Synergy Global Hospital, a multispecialty healthcare facility located in Ranchi, Jharkhand. The research aims to identify operational wastes, analyse patient flow patterns, measure length of stay metrics, and develop a comprehensive Lean implementation framework tailored to the Indian hospital context.

**Methodology:** A mixed-methods approach was adopted, combining quantitative analysis of patient operational data (n=381 patients across four months: November 2025 to February 2026) with qualitative observations gathered during the internship period (August–October 2025). Data analysis encompassed descriptive statistics, trend analysis, value stream mapping, and comparative assessment against established benchmarks. Tools including 5S workplace organization, Value Stream Mapping (VSM), Kaizen events, and Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles were evaluated for applicability.

**Key Findings:** The analysis revealed an average length of stay of 3.29 days with significant variation across patient categories. Same-day discharges constituted 7.5 per cent of total cases, while stays exceeding five days accounted for 18.9 per cent. Monthly trends indicated a gradual increase in average LOS from 3.03 days (November 2025) to 3.72 days (February 2026), suggesting potential process deterioration that Lean interventions could address. Consultant workload analysis revealed concentration of 40 per cent of cases with a single physician, indicating resource imbalance. Value stream mapping identified non-value-added waiting time comprising approximately 35 per cent of the total patient journey.

**Conclusion:** The study demonstrates that Lean management principles offer substantial potential for improving hospital operations in the Indian healthcare context. A phased implementation framework incorporating 5S, VSM, Kaizen, and PDSA cycles is recommended. The findings contribute to the limited body of knowledge on Lean healthcare in Indian tier-two city hospitals and provide a replicable model for similar institutions.

**Keywords:** Lean Management, Hospital Operations, Value Stream Mapping, Length of Stay, Patient Flow, Quality Improvement, Healthcare Waste Elimination, Indian Healthcare, Kaizen, PDSA

### 1.2 General Introduction to the Topic

#### 1.2.1 Understanding Lean Management

Lean management represents a comprehensive philosophy of organizational improvement that originated in the manufacturing sector, specifically within the Toyota Motor Corporation during the post-World War II era in Japan. The term "Lean" was first coined by Krafcik (1988) and subsequently popularized by Womack, Jones, and Roos (1990) in their seminal work "The Machine That Changed the World." At its core, Lean management focuses on maximizing customer value while minimizing waste, thereby creating more value for customers with fewer resources (Womack and Jones, 1996).

The fundamental premise of Lean thinking rests upon five key principles: (1) defining value from the customer's perspective; (2) mapping the value stream to identify all steps in the process and eliminating those that do not create value; (3) creating flow so that value-creating steps occur in a tight sequence; (4) establishing pull so that nothing is produced upstream until the downstream customer signals a need; and (5) pursuing perfection through continuous improvement (Womack and Jones, 1996; Radnor and Williams, 2025).

#### 1.2.2 Lean Management in Healthcare

The adaptation of Lean principles to healthcare settings began in earnest during the early 2000s, with pioneering institutions such as Virginia Mason Medical Center in Seattle and ThedaCare in Wisconsin demonstrating that manufacturing-derived improvement methodologies could be successfully translated to clinical environments (Toussaint and Berry, 2013). In the healthcare context, the "customer" is the patient, and "value" is defined as any activity that directly contributes to improving the patient's health status or experience of care.

Healthcare operations present unique challenges for Lean implementation due to the inherent complexity of clinical processes, the variability of patient presentations, the critical nature of time-sensitive decisions, and the involvement of multiple professional disciplines in patient care delivery (Radnor et al., 2012; Brandao de Souza, 2009). Nevertheless, systematic reviews have consistently demonstrated that Lean interventions in hospitals can significantly reduce waiting times, shorten length of stay, improve throughput, decrease costs, and enhance both patient and staff satisfaction (Wang et al., 2025; D'Andreamatteo et al., 2015; Moraros et al., 2016).

#### 1.2.3 Relevance in the Indian Healthcare Context

India's healthcare system faces a distinctive set of challenges that make Lean management particularly relevant. The country contends with a dual burden of communicable and non-communicable diseases, an overburdened public health infrastructure, significant urban-rural disparities in healthcare access, and resource constraints that necessitate innovative approaches to doing more with less (Balarajan et al., 2011; Sengupta and Nundy, 2005).

The Indian hospital sector has witnessed rapid growth in the private sector over the past two decades, with private hospitals now accounting for approximately 62 per cent of inpatient care and 70 per cent of outpatient visits (National Health Accounts Estimates, 2019). However, many of these institutions, particularly in tier-two and tier-three cities, operate without formal process improvement frameworks, relying instead on ad hoc management approaches that often result in operational inefficiencies, resource wastage, and suboptimal patient experiences (Kaur et al., 2021; Parkhi, 2019).

Studies conducted in the Indian context have demonstrated that Lean and Six Sigma methodologies can yield significant improvements when adapted to local conditions. Research by Parkhi (2019) documented successful Lean Six Sigma implementation in an Indian tertiary care hospital, achieving measurable reductions in patient waiting times and process cycle times. Similarly, Bhat et al. (2024) reported that the Define-Measure-Analyse-Improve-Control (DMAIC) approach optimized workflow within six months at a large Indian academic hospital. However, the literature remains sparse regarding Lean implementation in hospitals located in smaller cities and resource-constrained environments (Kaur et al., 2021).

#### 1.2.4 The Hospital Operations Landscape

Hospital operations encompass the entire spectrum of activities that support patient care delivery, from front-desk registration and appointment scheduling through clinical assessment, diagnosis, treatment, and discharge. Each of these processes involves multiple stakeholders, information flows, physical movements, and decision points that collectively determine the efficiency, quality, and cost of care.

In the Indian context, common operational challenges include prolonged patient waiting times at outpatient departments, delays in diagnostic test reporting, inefficient bed management leading to extended lengths of stay, communication gaps between departments, inventory management issues in pharmacy and medical stores, and documentation bottlenecks that slow down discharge processes (Kanamori et al., 2016; Singh et al., 2022).

These challenges are not merely administrative inconveniences; they directly affect patient outcomes, satisfaction, and safety. Prolonged waiting times are associated with increased patient anxiety and dissatisfaction, delayed treatment initiation can worsen clinical outcomes, and inefficient discharge processes contribute to unnecessary bed occupancy that prevents new admissions and increases hospital-acquired infection risk (Vashi et al., 2019; Hussain and Malik, 2016).



### 1.3 Lean Management: Conceptual Framework

#### 1.3.1 The Five Principles of Lean Thinking

The conceptual foundation of Lean management rests upon five interconnected principles that collectively guide organizations toward operational excellence:

**1. Define Value:** In healthcare, value must be defined from the patient's perspective. Any activity that directly contributes to diagnosis, treatment, comfort, or recovery represents value. Conversely, activities that consume resources without adding to the patient's wellbeing constitute waste (Womack and Jones, 1996; Graban, 2018).

**2. Map the Value Stream:** Value stream mapping involves documenting every step in a process, distinguishing between value-adding activities, necessary non-value-adding activities (such as regulatory documentation), and pure waste. In hospital settings, this might involve mapping the entire patient journey from arrival to discharge (Rother and Shook, 2003; Alogla et al., 2025).

**3. Create Flow:** Once waste is removed, the remaining steps should flow smoothly without interruptions, batching, or delays. In hospitals, this translates to eliminating bottlenecks in patient movement, ensuring timely availability of test results, and coordinating interdepartmental handoffs (Graban, 2018).

**4. Establish Pull:** Services and resources should be provided based on actual patient demand rather than projected schedules. This principle advocates against overproduction and over-processing, encouraging just-in-time delivery of services (Womack and Jones, 1996).

**5. Pursue Perfection:** Lean is not a one-time project but a continuous journey of improvement. The pursuit of perfection involves engaging every member of the organization in identifying and eliminating waste on an ongoing basis (Liker, 2004; Toussaint and Berry, 2013).

#### 1.3.2 The Seven Wastes (Muda) in Healthcare

Taiichi Ohno, the architect of the Toyota Production System, identified seven categories of waste that have been adapted for healthcare application. An eighth waste (underutilized talent) was later added by contemporary Lean practitioners.

**Table 1.3: Seven Wastes (Muda) in Healthcare Context**

| Waste Type | Manufacturing Example | Healthcare Example |
|-----------|----------------------|-------------------|
| Overproduction | Making more than needed | Unnecessary lab tests, redundant documentation |
| Waiting | Idle time between steps | Patients waiting for appointments, test results, discharge |
| Transportation | Unnecessary movement of goods | Moving patients between departments, transporting specimens |
| Over-processing | More work than required | Excessive paperwork, duplicate data entry |
| Inventory | Excess stock | Overstocked medications, expired supplies |
| Motion | Unnecessary worker movement | Staff walking to distant supply rooms, searching for equipment |
| Defects | Rework and corrections | Medication errors, misdiagnoses, readmissions |
| Underutilized Talent | Not using worker skills | Nurses doing clerical work, doctors filling forms |

*Source: Adapted from Ohno (1988) and Graban (2018)*

#### 1.3.3 Key Lean Tools for Hospital Settings

Several specific Lean tools have demonstrated applicability in hospital environments:

**5S Methodology:** A workplace organization technique comprising Sort (remove unnecessary items), Set in Order (organize remaining items logically), Shine (clean and maintain), Standardize (create consistent procedures), and Sustain (maintain improvements through culture change). In hospitals, 5S has been applied to nursing stations, operating theatres, pharmacy stores, and medical records departments with documented improvements in efficiency and safety (Singh et al., 2022; Pandya et al., 2015).

**Value Stream Mapping (VSM):** A diagnostic tool that creates a visual representation of the entire process flow, documenting cycle times, waiting times, and information flows. In healthcare, VSM has been used to map emergency department patient journeys, surgical pathways, and outpatient clinic processes, consistently revealing significant non-value-added time (Alogla et al., 2025; Henrique et al., 2016).

**Kaizen (Continuous Improvement Events):** Short, focused improvement events (typically three to five days) that bring together cross-functional teams to address specific process problems. Kaizen events in hospitals have addressed issues ranging from discharge planning to medication administration processes (Shatrov et al., 2021; Mazzocato et al., 2010).

**Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) Cycles:** An iterative improvement methodology that involves planning a change, implementing it on a small scale, studying the results, and acting on the findings. PDSA is widely used in healthcare quality improvement and is endorsed by organizations including the Institute for Healthcare Improvement (Taylor et al., 2014; Langley et al., 2009).

**Kanban (Visual Management):** A system for controlling inventory and workflow using visual signals. In hospital pharmacy, Kanban cards can signal when stock levels reach reorder points, preventing both stockouts and overstocking (Graban, 2018).

**Standard Work:** Documenting the current best practice for performing a task, ensuring consistency and providing a baseline against which improvements can be measured. Standard work in nursing, for example, has been shown to reduce variability in patient care processes and improve safety (Kim et al., 2006).

## CHAPTER 2: OBJECTIVES, SCOPE, AND PURPOSE OF THE STUDY

### 2.1 Objectives of the Study

The objectives of this research project are defined with specificity and measurability, guided by the SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) framework:

6. **To evaluate the potential impact of Lean interventions** on key performance indicators by comparing current operational metrics against published benchmarks from Lean-implemented hospitals in similar settings.

7. **To generate evidence-based recommendations** for phased Lean implementation that account for resource constraints, staff readiness, and organizational culture specific to the Indian healthcare context.

**Secondary Objectives:**

8. To assess the readiness of hospital staff and management for Lean adoption by documenting existing improvement practices and identifying potential barriers to implementation.

9. To contribute to the academic literature on Lean healthcare in India by providing a replicable methodology and documented findings suitable for journal publication.

10. To develop the researcher's competencies in healthcare operations analysis, data-driven decision-making, and quality improvement methodology applicable to future roles in healthcare consulting, administration, and doctoral research.

### 2.2 Scope of the Study

#### 2.2.1 Coverage and Boundaries

**Geographical Scope:** This study is confined to Synergy Global Hospital, Ranchi, Jharkhand, India. While findings may be generalizable to similar institutions, direct applicability is limited to the studied hospital and comparable settings.

**Temporal Scope:** Primary data collection covers a four-month period from November 2025 to February 2026, with contextual observations gathered during the three-month internship period (August–October 2025). The combined seven-month period provides both qualitative process understanding and quantitative operational data.

**Departmental Scope:** The study encompasses the entire inpatient journey, cutting across multiple departments including the admission counter, inpatient wards, nursing stations, diagnostic services, pharmacy, and discharge counter. The outpatient department is addressed only insofar as it feeds into inpatient admissions.

**Sample Scope:** The quantitative analysis is based on 381 inpatient records (complete census of admissions during the study period) and 317 consultant-linked patient records, providing comprehensive coverage rather than a sample-based approach.

**Methodological Scope:** The study applies established Lean tools (5S assessment, Value Stream Mapping, waste identification) in an analytical capacity. It does not implement actual Lean interventions during the study period but rather diagnoses the current state and proposes future interventions.

#### 2.2.2 Limitations and Exclusions

The following aspects are explicitly excluded from the scope of this study:

- Financial analysis of hospital profitability or detailed cost accounting
- Clinical outcome assessment (morbidity, mortality, complication rates)
- Outpatient department operations as a standalone analysis
- Comparison with other hospitals in the region
- Implementation and measurement of actual Lean interventions (the study is diagnostic and prescriptive in nature)
- Staff satisfaction surveys or formal interview-based qualitative research
- Patient satisfaction measurement through structured instruments

**Assumptions:**

## CHAPTER 3: REVIEW OF LITERATURE

### 3.1 Evolution of Lean in Healthcare

#### 3.1.1 Origins and Historical Development

The genesis of Lean thinking is inextricably linked to the Toyota Production System (TPS) developed by Taiichi Ohno and Shigeo Shingo in post-war Japan (Ohno, 1988). Faced with severe resource constraints and the need to compete with Western mass production, Toyota engineers developed a production philosophy centered on waste elimination, continuous improvement, and respect for people. This philosophy was formalized and disseminated to Western audiences through the work of Womack et al. (1990) and subsequently refined into the five Lean principles by Womack and Jones (1996).

The migration of Lean thinking from manufacturing to healthcare began tentatively in the late 1990s and accelerated during the 2000s. Spear (2005) articulated how the principles underlying TPS could apply to healthcare delivery, arguing that hospitals, like factories, are systems comprising interconnected processes that transform inputs into outputs. The critical insight was that healthcare waste—unnecessary waiting, redundant testing, medication errors, inefficient workflows—was conceptually equivalent to manufacturing waste, and similar systematic approaches could address it.

Early adopters included Virginia Mason Medical Center (Kenney, 2011), which launched its comprehensive "Virginia Mason Production System" in 2002, and ThedaCare (Toussaint, 2014), which demonstrated organization-wide Lean transformation. In the United Kingdom, the National Health Service (NHS) embraced Lean through initiatives such as the "Productive Ward: Releasing Time to Care" programme, which was implemented in over 70 per cent of NHS trusts and demonstrated measurable improvements in direct care time (NHS Institute for Innovation and Improvement, 2011).

#### 3.1.2 Theoretical Foundations

The theoretical underpinning of Lean healthcare draws from multiple disciplines:

**Operations Management Theory:** The fundamental premise that healthcare delivery can be understood and improved through the same operations management principles applicable to any service system (Slack et al., 2019). This includes concepts of process design, capacity management, quality control, and inventory optimization.

**Systems Thinking:** Recognition that hospitals are complex adaptive systems where interventions in one area can have unintended consequences elsewhere. Lean's value stream perspective explicitly addresses this by examining end-to-end processes rather than isolated departments (Seddon, 2008; Mazzocato et al., 2010).

**Human Factors and Ergonomics:** Lean's emphasis on standard work, visual management, and error-proofing (Poka-Yoke) draws heavily from human factors science, recognizing that system design—not individual blame—is the primary determinant of performance reliability (Graban, 2018).

**Change Management Theory:** Successful Lean implementation requires attention to organizational culture, leadership commitment, and staff engagement. Theories including Kotter's eight-step model and the ADKAR framework provide complementary guidance for managing the human dimensions of Lean transformation (van Rossum et al., 2016; Radnor et al., 2012).

#### 3.1.3 Phases of Lean Healthcare Adoption

The global adoption of Lean in healthcare can be characterized in three broad phases:

**Phase 1 (2000–2010): Pioneering and Experimentation.** Characterized by isolated tool application (typically 5S and VSM) in selected departments, often led by external consultants. Results were frequently impressive but sustainability was poor, as organizational culture and management systems were not aligned (Radnor et al., 2012).

**Phase 2 (2010–2020): Systematization and Evidence Building.** Marked by attempts at system-wide Lean implementation, development of Lean management systems (daily management, leader standard work), and increasing academic scrutiny through systematic reviews and meta-analyses (D'Andreamatteo et al., 2015; Moraros et al., 2016; Hallam and Contreras, 2018).

**Phase 3 (2020–Present): Integration and Digital Enhancement.** Current trends involve integrating Lean with digital health technologies (process mining, predictive analytics, AI-driven decision support), adapting Lean for post-pandemic healthcare resilience, and embedding Lean principles within broader frameworks such as value-based healthcare and population health management (Brancalion et al., 2024; MDPI Healthcare, 2025).



### 3.2 Lean Tools and Techniques in Hospital Settings

#### 3.2.1 Five-S (5S) in Healthcare

The 5S methodology has been one of the most widely adopted Lean tools in hospital settings, primarily because of its visual nature, relative simplicity, and ability to produce quick, tangible results that build momentum for broader Lean initiatives. Singh et al. (2022) conducted an implementation study of 5S in a clinical biochemistry laboratory of a government hospital in India, documenting improvements in turnaround time, reduction of search time for reagents, and enhanced workplace safety compliance. Similarly, Pandya et al. (2015) evaluated the implementation of a "5S Campaign" in an urban health centre in Gujarat, India, reporting significant improvements in workplace organization scores and staff satisfaction with the working environment.

A systematic review by Kanamori et al. (2016) examining 5S implementation across healthcare settings in multiple countries concluded that the methodology consistently improves workplace organization and safety, but emphasized that sustainability requires integration with broader management systems rather than treatment as a standalone project. The review identified leadership commitment, regular auditing, and staff ownership as critical success factors.

The 6S extension (adding Safety as the sixth S) has gained traction in healthcare, recognizing the unique patient safety implications of workplace disorganization in clinical environments. A recent study by PMC (2024) documented 6S implementation in a primary health centre in India, demonstrating measurable improvements in workflow efficiency and infection control compliance.

#### 3.2.2 Value Stream Mapping in Hospital Processes

Value Stream Mapping has emerged as the cornerstone diagnostic tool for Lean healthcare initiatives. By creating a visual representation of both material and information flows across an entire process, VSM enables teams to see waste that might otherwise remain invisible within departmental silos.

Alogla et al. (2025), writing in Frontiers in Health Services, revisited the application of VSM from manufacturing to healthcare settings and found that contextualized implementation—adapting VSM symbols and metrics to healthcare realities—produced substantial reductions in patient waiting time without compromising value-added care activities. The authors emphasized that healthcare VSM must account for the inherent variability of patient presentations, distinguishing between "necessary variation" (clinical complexity) and "unnecessary variation" (process failure).

Henrique et al. (2016) conducted a systematic review of VSM applications in healthcare and identified that the most successful implementations shared common features: multidisciplinary team involvement in mapping exercises, inclusion of patients in defining value, and explicit measurement of time spent in each activity. Studies reporting VSM application to emergency department patient flows documented lead time reductions ranging from 11 to 88 per cent (Wang et al., 2025).

In a recent application relevant to the Indian context, research at a Polish neurological institute (PMC, 2025) demonstrated that VSM could identify that only 22 per cent of the total patient journey time was spent on actual value-adding clinical activities, with the remaining 78 per cent consumed by waiting, transportation, and administrative processes.

#### 3.2.3 Kaizen and Rapid Improvement Events

Kaizen, the Japanese term for continuous improvement, has been adapted to healthcare through two primary modalities: "kaizen events" (intensive improvement workshops lasting three to five days) and "daily kaizen" (ongoing incremental improvements embedded in routine work).

Shatrov et al. (2021), in their PLOS One publication examining kaizen implementation across acute care hospitals, identified several factors critical for successful implementation: strong managerial support, availability of dedicated resources for improvement work, team cohesion across professional hierarchies, and clear connection between improvement activities and organizational goals. Their research found that kaizen was most effective when frontline staff were empowered to identify and solve problems within their own work areas, rather than having solutions imposed by management.

A mixed-methods study by BMJ Open (2023) assessed the sustainability of performance improvements following 26 kaizen events at a large academic hospital system. The findings revealed that while immediate improvements were consistently achieved, long-term sustainability (beyond six months) was observed in only 60 per cent of cases. Factors associated with sustained improvements included embedding changes in standard work documentation, assigning process ownership, and conducting regular follow-up auditing.

Al-Hakim et al. (2023) proposed a practical guide to the kaizen approach as a quality improvement tool, specifically for healthcare practitioners. Their framework emphasized the importance of visual management boards, regular huddles, and structured problem-solving using the A3 methodology to make kaizen accessible to clinical staff who may lack formal Lean training.

#### 3.2.4 Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) Cycles

The PDSA cycle, while not exclusively a Lean tool, is widely employed within Lean healthcare implementations as the preferred methodology for testing and refining improvements. Taylor et al. (2014), in their systematic review published in BMJ Quality and Safety, examined the application of PDSA in healthcare quality improvement and found widespread use but frequently poor methodological fidelity. Common shortcomings included failure to make predictions before testing, inadequate data collection during the "Study" phase, and jumping to full-scale implementation without iterative small-scale testing.

The StatPearls resource on Quality Improvement Methods (2024) provides a comprehensive overview of PDSA alongside Lean and Six Sigma, noting that PDSA's iterative format makes it particularly suitable for healthcare environments where large-scale changes carry patient safety risks. The resource emphasizes that PDSA should be applied to small, testable changes initially, with scale-up occurring only after positive results are consistently demonstrated.

Reed and Card (2016) argued that effective PDSA requires what they term "scientific rigour in improvement science"—clear hypotheses, specified measures, and honest evaluation of results even when outcomes are disappointing. Their work has influenced contemporary practice guidelines that emphasize documentation of each PDSA cycle's learning, not merely its outcomes.

#### 3.2.5 Lean Six Sigma Integration

The integration of Lean with Six Sigma represents a powerful hybrid approach that combines Lean's focus on waste elimination and flow with Six Sigma's emphasis on defect reduction and statistical process control. In healthcare, this combination addresses both efficiency (Lean) and quality (Six Sigma) objectives simultaneously.

Bhat et al. (2024), in their study published in Emerald's International Journal of Quality and Reliability Management, documented the application of Lean Six Sigma in an Indian tertiary care hospital using action research methodology. The DMAIC framework was employed to optimise workflow, resulting in significant process improvements within six months. Their findings highlighted that the structured problem-solving approach of DMAIC, combined with Lean's waste elimination focus, created synergistic improvements exceeding what either methodology alone would have achieved.

Improta et al. (2023) reported on Lean Six Sigma application in a Brazilian intensive care unit, demonstrating a 61 per cent reduction in mean transfer time through systematic process analysis and redesign. This study exemplified the methodology's applicability to critical care environments where both efficiency and safety are paramount.



### 3.3 Empirical Evidence from Global Studies

#### 3.3.1 Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses

The most comprehensive recent systematic review of Lean implementation in hospitals was conducted by Wang et al. (2025) and published in the International Journal of Health Policy and Management. Screening 6,021 articles and including 60 studies, the review synthesized evidence across four thematic dimensions: efficiency, quality, cost, and satisfaction. Key findings included that 49 studies documented efficiency improvements, with waiting time, length of stay, and patient volume being the most commonly measured variables. Seventeen studies examined cost reductions, with operating cost being the most frequently addressed metric, and 12 studies reported quality improvements across 12 sub-dimensions.

D'Andreamatteo et al. (2015), in an earlier but highly cited review, examined 243 articles on Lean in healthcare and found that emergency departments, operating rooms, and laboratories were the most common settings for Lean interventions. The review noted a geographical concentration of published studies in the United States, United Kingdom, and Australia, with limited representation from developing countries.

Moraros et al. (2016) conducted a systematic review specifically examining the effectiveness of Lean interventions in healthcare. Their analysis revealed that while many studies reported positive outcomes, the overall quality of evidence was moderate, with most studies employing pre-post designs without control groups. They called for more rigorous research designs including randomized controlled trials where ethically feasible.

A recent scoping review by BMC Health Services Research (2025) on sustainable care quality improvement through Lean healthcare found that a significant barrier to sustainability is the limitation of monitoring systems for process performance, reinforcing the importance of dashboard-based performance tracking systems as proposed in the current study.

#### 3.3.2 Emergency Department Studies

Emergency departments have been particularly fertile ground for Lean interventions due to their inherent time pressure, high variability, and direct impact of delays on patient outcomes.

Souza et al. (2021) conducted a systematic review of Lean applications in emergency departments, documenting consistent improvements in door-to-doctor time, patient throughput, left-without-being-seen rates, and overall length of stay. The most commonly applied tools included VSM, 5S, visual management, and standard work.

Yun et al. (2022) evaluated the impact of a new bed assignment information system (a Lean intervention) on emergency department length of stay, using interrupted time series and propensity score matching analysis. The rigorous methodology demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in ED boarding time, providing strong evidence for the effectiveness of Lean-informed system redesign.

Hussein et al. (2025), in their study of emergency nursing care post-pandemic, demonstrated that Lean approaches can simultaneously reduce patient wait times and improve both patient and staff satisfaction even in the challenging context of post-COVID healthcare delivery.

#### 3.3.3 Surgical and Inpatient Studies

Lean interventions in surgical and inpatient settings have focused primarily on reducing length of stay, improving bed turnover, and optimizing discharge processes.

A study by Springer (2024) analyzed the application of Lean Six Sigma tools to reduce patient length of stay in inpatient settings at tertiary hospitals. Using improved care progression and discharge planning processes, the researchers achieved an average reduction of 0.6 days in one hospital and over one day in another, with concurrent improvements in 30-day readmission rates. These findings are particularly relevant to the current study given the similar institutional context.

The NHS Productive Ward programme (later known as "Releasing Time to Care") demonstrated that Lean principles could increase the proportion of nursing time spent on direct patient care from approximately 40 per cent to 60 per cent, primarily by eliminating waste in documentation, supply retrieval, and communication processes (NHS Institute, 2011; Wilson, 2009).

#### 3.3.4 Pharmacy and Laboratory Studies

PMC (2024) published a systematic review of Lean and Six Sigma strategies in hospital pharmacy operations, documenting improvements in dispensing time, medication error rates, and inventory management efficiency across 26 studies globally. The review noted increased research output during the COVID-19 pandemic, suggesting that the crisis catalysed interest in efficiency methodologies.

In laboratory settings, Lean interventions have focused on reducing turnaround time for test results—a critical metric given its direct impact on treatment decisions and length of stay. Studies have demonstrated turnaround time reductions of 20–50 per cent through VSM-guided process redesign, batch size reduction, and workflow standardization (Clark et al., 2013; Lighter, 2014).



### 3.4 Lean Healthcare in the Indian Context

#### 3.4.1 Current State of Lean Adoption in India

The adoption of Lean management in Indian healthcare remains in a relatively nascent stage compared to Western nations. Kaur et al. (2021), in their study published in PLOS One, surveyed 109 hospitals across India and found that only 15 had implemented Lean tools while 14 had implemented Six Sigma tools. This low adoption rate was attributed to lack of awareness among healthcare administrators, shortage of trained Lean practitioners, resource constraints for training and implementation, and scepticism regarding the applicability of manufacturing-derived methods to healthcare.

Citybabu and Jayakrishna (2022), in their review published in The TQM Journal, examined the implementation of Lean Six Sigma in the Indian context and identified critical success factors including top management commitment, training infrastructure, project selection rigour, and cultural readiness. They highlighted that Indian organizations often face unique challenges related to hierarchical organizational structures, resistance to transparency, and limited data maturity that complicate Lean adoption.

Antony et al. (2022) presented strategies for successful deployment and sustainment of Lean Six Sigma in the Indian healthcare sector from a multi-level perspective. Their framework addressed individual (skills, motivation), organizational (leadership, culture), and systemic (policy, ecosystem) factors that collectively determine implementation success. The multi-level approach is particularly insightful for the Indian context where healthcare governance involves interactions between private providers, government regulators, and insurance intermediaries.

#### 3.4.2 Case Studies from Indian Hospitals

Several documented case studies provide evidence of Lean effectiveness in Indian hospital settings:

**Medanta – The Medicity (Gurgaon):** One of India's earliest Lean healthcare adopters, Medanta implemented Lean principles in its emergency department and surgical services, reporting significant reductions in patient waiting times and improved surgical throughput (documented in grey literature and conference proceedings).

**Narayana Health (Bangalore):** While not explicitly branded as "Lean," Narayana Health's model of high-volume, low-cost cardiac surgery exemplifies several Lean principles including standardization, flow optimization, and waste elimination. The hospital performs heart surgeries at approximately one-tenth the cost of comparable procedures in developed nations (Khanna et al., 2005).

**Bhat et al. (2024) – Indian Tertiary Academic Hospital:** This action research study implemented the full DMAIC cycle in a large Indian academic hospital, documenting workflow optimization achieved within six months. The study is notable for its rigorous methodology and explicit attention to change management challenges specific to the Indian healthcare culture.

**Parkhi (2019) – Indian Multispecialty Hospital:** This study documented Lean Six Sigma implementation through a change management approach, emphasizing that sustainability required embedding improvement processes within routine management systems rather than treating them as episodic projects.

#### 3.4.3 Challenges Specific to Indian Healthcare

Several challenges are particularly prominent in the Indian context:

**Hierarchical Culture:** Indian hospitals often maintain rigid hierarchies that inhibit the bottom-up communication and cross-functional teamwork essential to Lean (Sunder, 2016). Physicians may resist process standardization that they perceive as constraining clinical autonomy.

**Data Infrastructure:** Many Indian hospitals, particularly smaller private institutions, lack robust electronic health record systems and process data collection mechanisms that provide the foundation for evidence-based improvement (Kaur et al., 2021).

**Resource Constraints:** Dedicating staff time to improvement activities, investing in training, and potentially disrupting revenue-generating operations during change implementation poses financial challenges for smaller hospitals operating on thin margins.

**Workforce Dynamics:** High staff turnover, particularly among nursing and support staff, makes sustainability of improvements challenging as institutional knowledge is lost with departing personnel.

**Regulatory Environment:** Unlike in some Western countries where quality improvement is mandated and supported by regulatory frameworks, Indian healthcare regulation remains evolving, providing less external impetus for systematic improvement approaches.

#### 3.4.4 Opportunities for Lean in Indian Healthcare

Despite these challenges, several factors create favourable conditions for Lean adoption in India:

- Growing competition in the private healthcare sector creates market incentives for efficiency and quality improvement
- Increasing health insurance penetration (particularly Ayushman Bharat) creates pressure for cost containment while maintaining quality
- Young, technology-savvy healthcare workforce in India is potentially more receptive to systematic improvement methodologies
- National Accreditation Board for Hospitals (NABH) standards increasingly reference continuous quality improvement principles
- Government emphasis on healthcare quality through programmes like LaQshya (Labour Room Quality Improvement Initiative) creates awareness of improvement methodologies



### 3.5 Research Gaps Identified

Based on the comprehensive review of existing literature, the following research gaps have been identified that this study aims to address:

**Gap 1: Limited Studies in Tier-Two City Hospitals.** The overwhelming majority of published Lean healthcare research from India emanates from large metropolitan hospitals or academic medical centres. There is a significant absence of documented Lean analysis in hospitals located in smaller cities where contextual factors—patient demographics, resource availability, staff capabilities, competitive dynamics—differ substantially from metropolitan settings.

**Gap 2: Lack of Comprehensive Operational Data Analysis.** Many existing Indian studies rely on qualitative case descriptions or limited quantitative metrics. There is a need for studies that systematically analyse complete operational datasets (census-level admission data) to characterize hospital performance and identify improvement opportunities using data-driven approaches.

**Gap 3: Absence of Integrated Framework for Indian Context.** While individual Lean tools have been studied in isolation, there is a gap in literature providing integrated implementation frameworks that address the specific challenges of the Indian healthcare environment—hierarchical culture, limited data infrastructure, resource constraints, and workforce dynamics.

**Gap 4: Limited Dashboard and Visual Management Research.** Despite the growing importance of data visualization in healthcare management, there is sparse literature documenting the development and implementation of operational dashboards specifically designed for Lean performance monitoring in Indian hospitals.

**Gap 5: Jharkhand-Specific Healthcare Operations Research.** Healthcare operations research from the state of Jharkhand is virtually non-existent in published literature, despite the state's significant healthcare challenges and the presence of numerous private hospitals that could benefit from systematic improvement approaches.

## CHAPTER 4: RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

### 4.1 Research Design

This study employs a **descriptive-analytical research design** combining quantitative data analysis with qualitative process observation. The choice of this design is guided by the exploratory nature of the research objectives—to understand current operational patterns, identify wastes, and propose improvements—rather than to test the effectiveness of implemented interventions.

The research approach can be characterized along several dimensions:

**Nature of Research:** Applied research, aimed at generating practical solutions for a specific organizational context while contributing to academic knowledge.

**Research Philosophy:** Pragmatist, combining positivist quantitative analysis with interpretive qualitative observations to construct a comprehensive understanding of hospital operations.

**Research Approach:** Primarily deductive, applying established Lean frameworks (theory) to the specific context (practice) while remaining open to inductive insights emerging from data patterns.

**Time Horizon:** Cross-sectional analysis of four-month operational data, supplemented by longitudinal observations during the three-month internship period.

**Strategy:** Single-site case study with embedded quantitative analysis. The case study approach is appropriate for investigating contemporary phenomena within their real-life context, particularly when the boundaries between phenomenon and context are not clearly evident (Yin, 2018).

### 4.2 Data Collection Methods

#### 4.2.1 Primary Data Sources

**1. Hospital Administrative Records:**
The primary quantitative data source comprises inpatient admission and discharge records maintained by the hospital's medical records department. Data was extracted for the period November 2025 to February 2026, encompassing 381 unique patient records across four monthly registers.

**2. Consultant-Patient Assignment Records:**
A separate dataset of 317 patient records linking each patient to their attending consultant physician was analysed to understand workload distribution patterns.

**3. Process Observation:**
During the three-month internship period (August–October 2025), structured observations of hospital processes were conducted, documenting:
- Patient journey touchpoints from admission to discharge
- Waiting time patterns at various stages
- Communication flows between departments
- Supply chain and inventory management practices
- Discharge process steps and duration
- Workplace organization status across departments

**4. Informal Discussions:**
Conversations with hospital staff (consultants, nurses, administrators, support staff) provided contextual understanding of operational challenges, existing improvement practices, and organizational culture regarding change.

#### 4.2.2 Secondary Data Sources

- Published literature on Lean healthcare implementation
- National health statistics and policy documents
- Hospital promotional materials and website information
- State health department reports for Jharkhand
- NABH standards and guidelines

### 4.3 Sampling Technique

This study employs a **census approach** rather than sampling for quantitative analysis. All 381 inpatient records during the study period were included in the analysis, eliminating sampling bias and providing a complete picture of hospital operations during the data collection period.

For consultant workload analysis, all 317 available records with consultant information were included.

The selection of the study period (November 2025 – February 2026) was determined by data availability and represents a four-month window that captures both typical operational patterns and seasonal variation (post-monsoon through winter months).

**Inclusion Criteria:**
- All patients admitted as inpatients during November 2025 to February 2026
- Records with valid admission date
- Records contained in the official hospital patient register

**Exclusion Criteria:**
- Outpatient visits not resulting in admission
- Records with illegible or missing critical fields
- Day-care procedures not involving formal admission



### 4.4 Tools for Data Analysis

#### 4.4.1 Quantitative Analysis Tools

**Descriptive Statistics:** Mean, median, mode, standard deviation, range, and quartile analysis were computed for key variables including length of stay, daily admission volumes, and consultant caseloads. These provide the foundational characterization of operational performance.

**Trend Analysis:** Monthly and weekly trends in admissions and length of stay were examined to identify patterns, seasonality, and potential deterioration or improvement over time.

**Frequency Distribution:** Length of stay was categorized into clinically meaningful groups (same-day, 1–2 days, 3–5 days, 6+ days) and frequency distributions were constructed to understand the case-mix composition.

**Pareto Analysis:** The 80/20 principle was applied to consultant workload data to identify concentration of cases and opportunities for workload rebalancing.

**Statistical Testing:** One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was employed to test for significant differences in mean length of stay across months. Chi-square tests were used to assess the significance of distribution patterns.

#### 4.4.2 Lean Analysis Tools

**Value Stream Mapping:** A current-state VSM was constructed based on process observations, documenting each step in the patient journey from admission to discharge, with estimated cycle times, waiting times, and handoff points.

**Waste Identification Matrix:** Each process step was evaluated against the seven wastes framework (transportation, inventory, motion, waiting, overproduction, over-processing, defects) to systematically identify waste categories and their relative magnitude.

**5S Assessment:** A baseline 5S audit was conducted for key work areas (nursing station, pharmacy, medical records) using a standardized scoring rubric (1–5 scale for each S dimension).

#### 4.4.3 Visualization and Dashboard Tools

An interactive HTML/JavaScript dashboard was developed using open-source charting libraries (Chart.js) to visualize key operational metrics. The dashboard enables dynamic filtering by month and displays real-time-style visualizations of admission trends, LOS patterns, and consultant workload distribution.

### 4.5 Ethical Considerations

This study adheres to strict ethical principles governing research involving patient data:

**1. Anonymization:** All patient identifying information (names, contact numbers) has been anonymized in analytical outputs. While the raw data contains patient names for administrative purposes, all published analyses, dashboard displays, and report figures present only aggregated or de-identified data.

**2. Institutional Permission:** The study was conducted with the knowledge and implicit permission of hospital administration during the formal internship period. Access to data was granted as part of the internship arrangement.

**3. Data Security:** Patient data files were stored on password-protected systems with access restricted to the researcher. No patient data was transmitted over unsecured channels.

**4. Purpose Limitation:** Data collected was used exclusively for academic research and quality improvement analysis. No clinical decisions were made based on this data, and no patient care was affected by the study.

**5. Beneficence:** The study aims to ultimately benefit patients by identifying operational improvements that could enhance care delivery efficiency and quality.

**6. Compliance:** The study complies with relevant provisions of the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) ethical guidelines for biomedical research involving human subjects, specifically regarding observational studies using routinely collected administrative data.

#### 5.5.2 Key Waste Categories Identified

**1. **2. Over-processing:** Multiple documentation of the same patient information across different registers (admission register, nursing notes, pharmacy records, billing system) without integrated electronic system. Redundant vitals documentation where nursing records duplicate what is already in monitoring equipment printouts.

**5. Inventory:** Pharmacy overstocking of certain medications while experiencing stockouts of others. Ward supply rooms containing expired or rarely-used items alongside critical supplies.

**6. Defects:** Incomplete discharge documentation requiring callback or rework. Incorrect billing entries requiring correction. Medication order transcription errors from handwritten prescriptions.

**7. Underutilized Talent:** Qualified nurses performing clerical tasks (form filling, phone calls to families) that could be handled by administrative staff. Senior physicians spending time on routine follow-ups that could be managed by junior doctors.

A future-state VSM incorporating Lean interventions would target:
- Reducing discharge decision waiting from 180 to 30 minutes through structured discharge rounds
- Eliminating batch processing in laboratory through continuous-flow sample handling
- Reducing documentation redundancy through standardized admission kits
- Implementing bedside registration to eliminate admission queue waiting
- Creating a discharge lounge to free beds immediately upon discharge decision

Target Process Cycle Efficiency: **45–50%** (achievable based on literature benchmarks)

## CHAPTER 6: FINDINGS AND DISCUSSION

### 6.1 Key Findings

The data analysis and process observations yield several significant findings that collectively paint a comprehensive picture of hospital operations at Synergy Global Hospital:

**Finding 1: Moderate Operational Efficiency with Improvement Potential**

The hospital's average LOS of 3.29 days positions it favourably compared to Indian private hospital averages (4.2 days) but above global Lean-optimized benchmarks (2.5–3.0 days). This suggests that while the hospital operates reasonably well, there exists quantifiable room for improvement that Lean interventions could capture. A target LOS reduction of 15–20 per cent (to approximately 2.7–2.8 days) appears achievable based on the literature and the identified wastes.

**Finding 2: Significant Process Waste in the Patient Journey**

Value stream mapping revealed that approximately 78.5 per cent of the inpatient journey time is consumed by non-value-adding activities. The three largest waste categories are:
- Waiting for physician availability (discharge decisions, initial consultation)
- Waiting for diagnostic results (batch processing effect)
- Redundant documentation and administrative processing

These wastes are addressable through Lean interventions without requiring significant capital investment—a critical consideration for resource-constrained institutions.

**Finding 3: Concerning Trend of Increasing LOS**

The progressive increase in average LOS from 3.03 days (November) to 3.72 days (February) represents a 23 per cent deterioration over four months. While not statistically significant in ANOVA testing (due to high within-group variability), the practical significance of this trend is substantial. If unchecked, this trajectory would result in approximately 400 additional bed-days consumed annually, equivalent to the throughput capacity for 120+ additional patients.

**Finding 4: Extreme Consultant Workload Concentration**

The finding that 40.1 per cent of all inpatient cases are managed by a single consultant represents a critical operational vulnerability. This concentration creates:
- Single-point-of-failure risk for hospital throughput
- Potential quality and safety concerns from physician overload
- Constraints on hospital growth independent of physical capacity
- Discharge timing dependencies on one physician's schedule

**Finding 5: Inverse Relationship Between Volume and Efficiency**

The negative correlation between monthly admission volumes and average LOS suggests that the hospital's discharge processes are influenced by occupancy pressure rather than standardized protocols. When beds are in demand (December: high admissions, low LOS), discharges occur efficiently. When demand decreases (January–February), discharge urgency diminishes and patients stay longer. This indicates absence of standardized discharge criteria and timing protocols.

**Finding 6: High Proportion of Short-Stay Cases Suitable for Pathway Standardization**

With 50 per cent of patients discharged within two days and 65 per cent within three days, there is a large cohort of cases suitable for standardized clinical pathways. Developing evidence-based care pathways for common conditions (with pre-defined discharge criteria) could simultaneously improve consistency, reduce variability, and enhance patient experience by setting clear expectations.

**Finding 7: Documentation and Data Quality Issues**

The 15.5 per cent rate of incomplete discharge records indicates weaknesses in documentation processes that compromise both operational visibility and quality of care records. In a Lean framework, this represents a "defect" waste category requiring systematic correction through standard work and verification protocols.



### 6.2 Discussion in Context of Literature

#### 6.2.1 Alignment with Global Lean Healthcare Evidence

The findings of this study are broadly consistent with the global body of evidence on Lean healthcare implementation. The process cycle efficiency of 21.5 per cent (meaning only about one-fifth of patient journey time is value-adding) aligns with observations from multiple international studies. Graban (2018) reports that healthcare processes typically contain 60–90 per cent waste, while the Polish VSM study (PMC, 2025) documented 78 per cent non-value time—closely matching this study's finding of 78.5 per cent.

The systematic review by Wang et al. (2025) found that the most common efficiency variables addressed by Lean interventions are waiting time, length of stay, and patient volumes—precisely the metrics where this study identifies improvement opportunities at Synergy Global Hospital. Their finding that 49 of 60 reviewed studies reported efficiency improvements provides confidence that similar gains are achievable in this context.

#### 6.2.2 Comparison with Indian Healthcare Studies

Kaur et al. (2021) reported that only 15 of 109 surveyed Indian hospitals had implemented Lean tools. Synergy Global Hospital, operating without formal Lean implementation, represents the majority of Indian hospitals that have not yet adopted systematic improvement methodologies. The operational patterns observed—high process variability, reliance on individual physician practices rather than standardized protocols, limited data utilization for decision-making—are characteristic of this pre-Lean state.

The achievable improvements documented by Bhat et al. (2024) in an Indian academic hospital (workflow optimization within six months using DMAIC) suggest that similar institutions can make rapid progress once systematic improvement methodologies are adopted. Importantly, their study demonstrated that Indian healthcare workers, when properly trained and supported, are receptive to Lean approaches despite the cultural challenges noted in the literature.

#### 6.2.3 Implications of Workload Concentration

The extreme concentration of cases with a single consultant (40.1 per cent) is not widely documented in published literature, likely because it represents a vulnerability that established institutions address early in their quality journey. However, it reflects a common reality in smaller Indian private hospitals where individual physicians often build personal patient followings that create institutional dependency.

From a Lean perspective, this concentration represents "Mura" (unevenness)—one of the three categories of waste in the Toyota framework alongside Muda (waste) and Muri (overburden). The physician handling 127 cases is likely overburdened (Muri), while other consultants are underutilized (Muda of talent), and the resulting uneven flow creates systemic inefficiency (Mura).

#### 6.2.4 The Volume-Efficiency Paradox

The inverse relationship between admission volume and LOS observed in this study resonates with findings from the broader hospital management literature. Studies have shown that hospitals operating closer to capacity tend to discharge patients faster—not necessarily because care is compromised, but because operational urgency drives process compliance. The phenomenon has been described as the "corridor patient effect" where visible overcrowding motivates faster decision-making (Bain et al., 2010).

However, relying on capacity pressure to drive efficiency is unsustainable and potentially unsafe. Lean philosophy advocates for building efficiency into the process design itself through standardized work, visual management, and daily improvement discipline—so that efficient operations persist regardless of current occupancy levels. This is precisely the transformation that the proposed framework aims to achieve.

#### 6.2.5 Role of Data and Dashboards

The study's findings reinforce the argument made by multiple authors (Wang et al., 2025; BMC Health Services Research, 2025) that data-driven monitoring is essential for sustained Lean performance. The operational patterns identified in this study—increasing LOS trend, workload concentration, documentation gaps—would likely remain invisible without systematic data analysis. The proposed interactive dashboard addresses this gap by making operational performance continuously visible to management.

## CHAPTER 7: RECOMMENDATIONS

### 7.1 Short-term Recommendations (0–3 Months)

Based on the findings of this study, the following actionable recommendations are prioritized by impact and feasibility:

**Recommendation 1: Implement Structured Discharge Rounds**

*Current Problem:* Discharge decisions are delayed by an average of 180 minutes due to physician availability constraints.

*Proposed Solution:* Institute a daily structured discharge round at a fixed time (recommended: 10:00 AM) where each consultant reviews all inpatients and makes discharge/continue decisions for each patient. This simple scheduling intervention eliminates the primary source of discharge delay without requiring any system changes.

*Expected Impact:* 50–70 per cent reduction in discharge decision waiting time, potential LOS reduction of 0.3–0.5 days.

*Evidence:* Consistent with findings from NHS Productive Ward programme (Wilson, 2009) and multiple discharge improvement studies (Springer, 2024).

**Recommendation 2: Create a Discharge Planning Checklist**

*Current Problem:* Discharge involves multiple sequential steps (physician clearance, pharmacy, billing, nursing documentation) executed in an unstructured manner with frequent rework.

*Proposed Solution:* Develop a single-page discharge checklist that anticipates all requirements and initiates parallel processing. For example, pharmacy can prepare medications while billing is being finalized, rather than waiting for sequential completion.

*Expected Impact:* 30–40 per cent reduction in discharge processing time (from 90 minutes to 55 minutes).

**Recommendation 3: Address Documentation Completeness**

*Current Problem:* 15.5 per cent of records lack complete discharge information.

*Proposed Solution:* Implement a "no incomplete record" policy where the nursing station verifies all documentation fields before patient discharge. Use a simple checklist verification system.

*Expected Impact:* Reduction of incomplete records to below 2 per cent, improving data quality for future analysis and quality monitoring.

**Recommendation 4: Deploy the Operational Dashboard**

*Current Problem:* Hospital management lacks real-time visibility into operational metrics, making it impossible to detect trends or respond proactively to performance changes.

*Proposed Solution:* Implement the interactive dashboard developed as part of this study, with weekly data updates. Designate responsibility for data entry and establish a weekly review meeting where management discusses dashboard metrics.

*Expected Impact:* Enables evidence-based management decision-making, early detection of adverse trends, and accountability for performance.

**Recommendation 5: Initiate 5S in One Department**

*Current Problem:* Work areas observed during internship show opportunities for improved organization, particularly in pharmacy storage, nursing supply rooms, and medical records.

*Proposed Solution:* Select the pharmacy or central nursing station as a 5S pilot site. Conduct a two-day 5S event with staff participation, followed by weekly audits for sustainability.

*Expected Impact:* Improved workplace organization, reduced search time for supplies, enhanced staff satisfaction, and visible demonstration of Lean's quick wins to build organizational momentum.

### 7.2 Medium-term Recommendations (3–6 Months)

**Recommendation 6: Develop Standardized Clinical Pathways**

For the top five diagnoses/procedures by volume, develop standardized clinical pathways that specify:
- Expected length of stay
- Daily milestones for clinical progress
- Pre-defined discharge criteria
- Patient education timelines

This addresses the high LOS variability (CV = 87.2%) by reducing unnecessary practice variation while preserving appropriate clinical judgment for complex cases.

**Recommendation 7: Address Consultant Workload Imbalance**

Engage in strategic discussions with the consultant team regarding case distribution. Possible approaches include:
- Developing a structured referral protocol that distributes emergency admissions more evenly
- Recruiting additional consultants in the overburdened specialty
- Creating a hospitalist model where a dedicated inpatient physician manages routine follow-up care
- Implementing a team-based care model with clear delegation protocols

**Recommendation 8: Reduce Laboratory Turnaround Time**

Conduct a focused Kaizen event targeting laboratory processes:
- Replace batch processing with continuous-flow sample handling
- Implement a Kanban system for sample prioritization
- Establish standard turnaround time targets with visual alerts for delays
- Consider point-of-care testing for frequently ordered basic investigations

**Recommendation 9: Implement Daily Huddles**

Institute brief (10-minute) daily huddles at the beginning of each shift where:
- Previous day's discharge status is reviewed
- Today's expected discharges are identified
- Potential barriers to discharge are proactively addressed
- Bed availability status is communicated
- Safety concerns are flagged

This practice, drawn from Lean daily management methodology, creates a proactive rather than reactive approach to patient flow management.

**Recommendation 10: Create Visual Management Boards**

Install simple visual management boards (whiteboards) at nursing stations displaying:
- Current bed occupancy
- Patients awaiting discharge
- Average LOS for the week (vs. target)
- Improvement ideas from staff
- Recognition of achieved improvements



### 7.3 Long-term Strategic Recommendations (6–12 Months and Beyond)

**Recommendation 11: Invest in Electronic Health Records (EHR)**

While representing a significant capital investment, transitioning from paper-based to electronic health records would address multiple identified wastes simultaneously:
- Eliminates redundant documentation (single entry, multiple access)
- Enables real-time data availability for dashboard automation
- Reduces transcription errors in medication ordering
- Facilitates clinical decision support and pathway compliance monitoring
- Provides the data infrastructure necessary for advanced Lean analytics

**Recommendation 12: Pursue NABH Entry-Level Certification**

NABH (National Accreditation Board for Hospitals) standards align naturally with Lean principles. Pursuing entry-level certification would:
- Provide external motivation for process standardization
- Create a structured framework for quality improvement
- Enhance hospital reputation and patient confidence
- Open doors to insurance panel empanelment
- Build documentation and audit capabilities essential for sustained Lean practice

**Recommendation 13: Develop Internal Lean Capability**

Rather than relying on external consultants for ongoing improvement:
- Sponsor two to three staff members for Lean Six Sigma Green Belt certification
- Create a formal Quality Improvement team with dedicated time allocation
- Establish a structured improvement project methodology (A3 thinking)
- Build a repository of improvement projects with documented methods and results

**Recommendation 14: Explore Demand Smoothing (Heijunka)**

Address the observed weekday variation in admissions by:
- Distributing planned surgical cases more evenly across the week
- Encouraging consultant OPD scheduling that spreads referrals
- Developing weekend/evening service options for diagnostic investigations
- Implementing planned admission scheduling with pre-admission testing to reduce day-of-admission workload

**Recommendation 15: Establish Patient Flow Centre**

For long-term operational excellence, create a centralized patient flow management function responsible for:
- Real-time bed management
- Discharge planning coordination
- Bottleneck identification and resolution
- Communication between departments regarding patient movement
- Data analysis and performance reporting

## CHAPTER 8: CONCLUSION

### 8.1 Summary of the Study

This research project set out to investigate the application of Lean management principles to hospital operations at Synergy Global Hospital, Ranchi, Jharkhand, through a comprehensive analysis of patient operational data and process observations gathered during a three-month internship period.

The study has achieved its stated objectives as follows:

**Objective 1 (Waste Identification):** Seven distinct waste categories were identified and documented through value stream mapping and process observation, with waiting time (accounting for approximately 78.5 per cent of non-clinical journey time) being the dominant waste form. Specific wastes included discharge delays, laboratory batch processing, documentation redundancy, and motion waste from facility layout.

**Objective 2 (Patient Flow and LOS Analysis):** Comprehensive analysis of 381 inpatient records revealed an average LOS of 3.29 days with a right-skewed distribution (median 2 days, maximum 18 days). The finding of increasing LOS trend over the study period (from 3.03 to 3.72 days) identifies a concerning efficiency trajectory.

**Objective 3 (Value Stream Mapping):** A complete current-state VSM of the inpatient journey was developed, revealing a process cycle efficiency of only 21.5 per cent—meaning less than one-quarter of the patient's time is spent on activities that directly contribute to their clinical improvement.

**Objective 4 (Lean Implementation Framework):** A phased, context-specific implementation framework was designed incorporating 5S, VSM, Kaizen, PDSA, and daily management system elements, with explicit attention to resource constraints and cultural factors of the Indian hospital context.

**Objective 5 (Performance Dashboard):** An interactive operational dashboard was developed and deployed, enabling visual monitoring of key metrics including admission trends, LOS patterns, and consultant workload distribution.

**Objective 6 (Benchmarking):** Comparison with published benchmarks confirmed that while Synergy Global Hospital performs within acceptable ranges for Indian private hospitals, there exists a measurable gap compared to Lean-optimized facilities that targeted interventions could close.

**Objective 7 (Recommendations):** Fifteen specific, evidence-based recommendations were developed spanning immediate quick wins through long-term strategic initiatives, each linked to specific findings and supported by literature evidence.

The study contributes to the limited body of research on Lean healthcare in Indian tier-two city hospitals, demonstrating that standard Lean diagnostic tools (VSM, waste analysis, data analysis) can be meaningfully applied in this context and generate actionable insights for operational improvement. The methodology and framework developed here offer a replicable template for similar institutions.

### 8.2 Limitations of the Study

This research acknowledges several limitations that should be considered when interpreting findings:

**1. Single-Site Study:** As a case study of one hospital, findings may not be directly generalizable to all Indian hospitals. However, the methodology is transferable.

**2. Observational Design:** Without implementing actual Lean interventions and measuring outcomes, the study cannot empirically validate the projected benefits of the proposed framework. The estimates of potential improvement are based on literature evidence from similar implementations elsewhere.

**3. Limited Data Granularity:** The available data comprised only admission dates, discharge dates, and consultant assignments. Detailed process timestamps (waiting times at each stage, specific procedure times) were not available in digital form, necessitating reliance on observational estimates for the value stream map.

**4. Short Duration:** The four-month data period, while providing a meaningful sample size (n=381), may not capture longer-term seasonal patterns or the full range of operational variation.

**5. No Patient Outcome Data:** Clinical outcomes, readmission rates, and patient satisfaction scores were not available for analysis, limiting the ability to correlate operational efficiency with care quality.

**6. Subjectivity in Process Observation:** Value stream mapping estimates are based on observational timing during the internship period and may contain measurement imprecision.

**7. Potential Data Quality Issues:** Some inconsistencies in the raw data (variant spellings of consultant names, apparent data entry errors in discharge dates) may introduce minor inaccuracies despite attempts at data cleaning.

### 8.3 Scope for Future Research

This study opens several avenues for future research:

**1. Longitudinal Implementation Study:** A natural extension would be to implement the proposed Lean framework and measure before-after outcomes over twelve to eighteen months, generating evidence of effectiveness in the Indian tier-two hospital context. This would be suitable for doctoral-level research.

**2. Multi-site Comparative Study:** Comparing Lean readiness and operational efficiency across multiple hospitals in Jharkhand or similar states would provide broader contextual understanding and identify common versus site-specific challenges.

**3. Patient Experience Integration:** Future research should incorporate structured patient satisfaction measurement to assess whether operational efficiency gains translate into improved patient-perceived quality of care.

**4. Economic Analysis:** A comprehensive cost-benefit analysis quantifying the financial impact of Lean interventions (reduced bed-days, improved throughput, decreased overtime) would strengthen the business case for hospital management investment.

**5. Staff Engagement and Culture Study:** Investigating the cultural and behavioural dimensions of Lean adoption in Indian healthcare—staff attitudes, physician receptivity, leadership styles—would complement the operational analysis presented here.

**6. Digital Lean Integration:** Exploring the application of process mining, artificial intelligence, and predictive analytics to enhance Lean practice in resource-constrained settings represents a cutting-edge research frontier.

**7. Policy Implications:** Research examining how state and national health policies could incentivize or support Lean adoption in private hospitals would inform healthcare governance reform.
